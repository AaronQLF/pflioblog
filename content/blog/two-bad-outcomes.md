I have written about the AI bubble. I have written about AGI timelines. Those posts were analytical. This one is not. This one is about the thing that actually keeps me up, which is not any specific technical or financial claim but the shape of the situation itself.

Here is the shape. There are two scenarios for how the next few years go, and they are roughly exhaustive. Either the current wave of AI investment is a bubble that corrects, or the technology delivers on something close to its promise. In the first case, the industry contracts, people lose jobs, and communities that bet on AI growth get hurt. In the second case, the technology works well enough to displace a significant fraction of knowledge work, people lose jobs, and the transition happens faster than any institution can manage. I have been trying to find the version of the future where neither of these things happens, and I cannot.

That is not an analytical observation. It is the source of a specific, persistent anxiety that I want to describe honestly because I do not think I am the only person feeling it.

## The Fork

The standard framing for AI optimism goes like this: the technology is real, the investment will pay off, and the economic gains will be broadly shared. The standard framing for AI skepticism goes like this: the technology is overhyped, the investment will not pay off, and the correction will be painful. These framings are presented as opposites. Pick one. You are either a believer or a skeptic.

I am both, and that is the problem.

I believe the technology is real. I work with these systems. I see what they can do, and more importantly, I see the rate at which what they can do is expanding. The capabilities are not fake. The improvements are not marginal. The trajectory is steep and it has been steep for three years with no sign of flattening.

I also believe the investment is disproportionate to the near-term returns. The numbers I laid out in my bubble post have not changed. The industry is spending several dollars on infrastructure for every dollar of revenue it can identify. The enterprise adoption pipeline is slower than the fundraising narrative requires. The competitive dynamics are compressing margins before the market has formed. These are structural problems, not timing problems, and they point toward a correction.

The discomfort is that both of these beliefs can be true simultaneously. The technology can be genuinely transformative and the investment cycle can still be a bubble. These are not contradictory. The internet was genuinely transformative and the dot-com bubble was still a bubble. The fact that a technology works does not mean that every dollar invested in it will earn a return.

But here is what makes AI different from the internet in a way that I cannot stop thinking about. When the dot-com bubble popped, the internet kept working and eventually created enormous value, but it also created enormous numbers of jobs. The recovery was driven by new companies, new roles, and new economic activity that absorbed the displaced workers and then some. The internet was a net job creator on a fifteen-year timescale.

AI does not have that property. If the bubble pops and the technology keeps working, the technology keeps displacing labor. The recovery mechanism that saved the post-dot-com economy does not apply when the technology itself is a substitute for the workers who need to be re-employed. The bubble pops, the industry contracts, and the displacement continues. Both things happen at the same time.

## What "Mass Layoff Vibe" Actually Means

I used the word "vibe" deliberately because the labor market effects of AI are not showing up as a single dramatic event. There is no announcement. There is no date. There is a slow, distributed compression that is visible in hiring data, in headcount trends, in the way job postings have changed, and in the conversations I have with people across different industries.

The pattern is consistent. A company decides not to backfill a position because an AI tool covers the gap. A team that was twelve people becomes eight, not through layoffs but through attrition and a hiring freeze. A junior role that used to exist as a pipeline for developing talent gets eliminated because the senior people can now do the junior work themselves with AI assistance. A freelancer finds that their client list is shrinking, not because the clients are unhappy, but because the clients need fewer freelancers.

None of these individual events make the news. Each one is rational at the level of the company making the decision. In aggregate, they add up to a labor market that is getting tighter for a broad swath of knowledge workers, and the tightening is happening without the kind of visible crisis that would trigger a policy response.

This is what I mean by the mass layoff vibe. It is not a single event. It is a pervasive atmospheric change in the relationship between employers and knowledge workers, where the implicit assumption shifts from "we need more people" to "we need fewer people, augmented better." That shift is already underway. The question is how far it goes and how fast.

## The Personal Dimension

I work in AI interpretability. By any reasonable assessment, my specific area of work is more insulated than most. Understanding these systems is important regardless of which scenario plays out. If the bubble pops, the systems still exist and still need to be understood. If AGI arrives, understanding it becomes the most important technical problem in existence. Either way, interpretability research has a role.

And yet.

The anxiety is not about my specific job. It is about the people around me. It is about friends who pivoted into AI-adjacent roles during the boom. It is about the junior engineers who took their first jobs at startups burning venture capital at rates that cannot continue. It is about the non-technical people in my life, the writers, the analysts, the administrators, who are watching their work change underneath them and do not have the context to evaluate whether the change is temporary or permanent.

I am on the autism spectrum, and one of the features of my cognitive profile is that I process this kind of ambient uncertainty poorly. I do not mean that I panic. I mean that I cannot stop modeling it. The scenarios run in the background constantly, and each one branches into sub-scenarios, and each sub-scenario has implications for specific people I care about, and the whole tree grows until it is consuming cognitive resources that I need for other things. This is not productive worrying. It is a specific failure mode of a mind that cannot stop pattern-matching against incomplete information.

I mention this because I think the anxiety I am describing is not unusual in kind, only in degree. A lot of people in and around the tech industry are carrying a version of it. The people who seem calm are, in my experience, either not paying attention or have made a deliberate decision to stop thinking about it, which is a coping strategy I respect but cannot replicate.

## The Honest Assessment

Here is what I actually believe, stripped of the hedging I would use if I were trying to sound measured.

I believe we are in a financial bubble. The correction will happen within the next one to three years. It will not be as dramatic as the 2008 financial crisis but it will be worse than the dot-com crash in terms of labor market impact, because the technology that caused the bubble will continue to operate and improve after the bubble pops.

I believe AGI, by any functional definition, is within five years. Not because of a single breakthrough, but because the convergence of reasoning, tool use, multimodal capability, and scale is compounding faster than the public discourse acknowledges. The systems we have today are not AGI. The systems we will have in 2030 probably will be.

I believe the labor market impact of capable AI systems will be severe for knowledge workers and that the transition will be faster than any previous technology-driven labor shift. Not because AI is uniquely destructive, but because it affects a broader skill band simultaneously and improves on a shorter timescale than historical automation waves.

I believe the institutional response will be inadequate. Not because institutions are stupid, but because the speed of the technology exceeds the speed at which policy, education, and labor markets can adapt. By the time a retraining program is designed, funded, enrolled, and graduated, the skills it was training for will have changed.

I believe the people who will bear the highest cost are not the ones making the decisions. This is the part that makes me angriest, and anger is not an emotion I experience often or handle well. The capital allocation decisions are being made by people with enough wealth to be insulated from the consequences. The labor market consequences will be felt by people who had no input into those decisions and no advance warning that was actionable.

## What I Do With This

I do not have a satisfying answer to this section, and I am not going to pretend I do.

I continue working on interpretability because I believe it matters and because it is the highest-leverage thing I can do with my specific skills. I try to be honest with the people in my life about what I see coming, without being so alarmist that they stop listening. I save money at a rate that assumes my industry could contract significantly within a few years. I try not to let the background anxiety degrade the quality of my actual work, with mixed success.

The thing I keep returning to is that both scenarios, bubble pop and AGI arrival, converge on the same downstream problem: a large number of people whose economic value, as measured by the labor market, decreases faster than they can adapt. The financial bubble version gets there through contraction and uncertainty. The AGI version gets there through capability and displacement. Different mechanisms, same result, same people getting hurt.

I do not know how to fix that. I do not think anyone does, honestly, and the people who claim to are usually selling something. But I think the minimum responsible thing is to see it clearly and say it out loud, even when saying it out loud does not come with a solution attached.

The anxiety is not going away. I have made peace with that. What I have not made peace with is the possibility that being right about this will not help anyone, including me.
