# Reality Is Not Given, It Is Built

I have been off. Not dramatically off, not in a way that would be visible from the outside. But internally there has been something like a static that makes it hard to hold a thought long enough to do anything serious with it. I sit down to read a paper and twenty minutes later I am somewhere else entirely, having not retained a single line. This has been going on for a few weeks. I have learned to stop fighting it and just let the cognition find its own footing.

When I am in states like this, I tend to do a lot of running and a lot of staring out windows. And something that happens in that kind of diffuse attention is that ideas I would normally skip past because they seem obvious start to feel genuinely strange. Like if you say a word too many times until it stops sounding like anything.

One of those ideas hit me pretty hard last week. Here it is:

**You are not perceiving reality. You are constructing it, continuously, using the least amount of actual incoming data you can get away with.**

---

## What Changed in How I Think About This

I have known the predictive processing framework in the abstract for a while. The idea that the brain is a prediction machine, not a passive receiver, is not new. But knowing something as a technical fact and actually updating your intuitions around it are two completely different things.

The specific thing that shifted was thinking about what happens when a prediction is violated.

When reality surprises you, your brain does not first perceive and then update. There is no raw perception stage. What you experience as the surprise, the jolt of the unexpected, is itself already a construction. The model throws up an error signal, and that signal is the moment of experience. You never get outside the model. The incoming photons, pressure waves, chemical gradients, whatever, they are always being interpreted through a prior, not received freshly.

This is not a metaphor. This is mechanistically what the visual cortex is doing. The descending projections from higher areas outnumber the ascending sensory fibers by something like 10 to 1 in some pathways. The brain is sending more information down toward the senses than it is receiving back up. You are mostly looking at a picture your brain already painted and making small corrections at the edges.

What hit me standing at the window was: **this means that reality, the one you live in, is principally a product of your priors**. Not your beliefs in the abstract sense. Your priors in the statistical sense. What you expect to see. What your model predicts will be there. That is the overwhelming majority of your visual field, your social world, your sense of what is possible, at any given moment.

---

## The Implication That Actually Disturbs Me

If this is right, then the question of what you experience is mostly a question of what you have habituated to predicting.

People who grew up in certain environments do not simply believe certain things about the world. Their perceptual apparatus predicts them. The predictions are encoded in the weighted connections, running automatically, prior to and beneath anything that feels like deliberate thought. You cannot argue someone out of this by providing better information, because the information is being filtered through the model before they consciously encounter it.

And the same is true of you. Whatever you think of as your objective reading of a situation, it is downstream of priors you cannot directly inspect. You have never perceived anything that was not already, at least partially, expected.

The disturbing part is not the philosophical implication in the abstract. The disturbing part is what this means about the feel of certainty. The moments when reality feels most solid, most obvious, most unambiguous, are the moments when your predictions are being most completely confirmed. High confidence in a model is not evidence that the model is accurate. It is evidence that the model has not been recently surprised. Those are very different things.

---

## A New Way I Am Thinking About Growth

This reframing changed something in how I think about what it means to change.

The usual model of personal growth is about acquiring knowledge or skills or discipline. The better version, I think, is that meaningful change is fundamentally about forcing your predictive apparatus to update in ways it would not have updated on its own.

This is why comfort is expensive. Not because comfort is morally suspect, but because a comfortable environment is by definition one where your predictions are mostly correct. Where your predictions are mostly correct, the update signal is small. The model is validated. It calculates, roughly speaking, that there is no need for revision.

Hard situations, novel environments, relationships with people who are structurally different from you, exposure to ideas that are genuinely not subsumable into your existing framework, these are not luxuries. They are the only mechanism by which the underlying predictive model gets revised in any deep sense.

What you choose to be exposed to is, in a quite direct mechanistic sense, what you choose to become.

---

## On the Fog Itself

I do not know why I have been unable to focus lately. I suspect it is a combination of overextension across too many domains and not enough of the kind of input that generates genuine update signals. The static might itself be a signal that the model is stale and the error correction has nowhere useful to go.

The thing I keep coming back to is this: even the difficulty focusing is a construction. The feeling that things are slippery, that attention keeps sliding off the surface of ideas, that too is a state my brain is generating based on some representation it has built. It is not passively receiving distraction. It is actively producing it.

I am not sure what to do with that yet. But it changes the character of the problem from something environmental, something being done to me, to something I am participating in. And that, at minimum, opens a different set of levers.
