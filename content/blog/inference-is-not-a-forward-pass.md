The standard mental model for running a large language model is three steps: load the weights, call `forward`, read the output. That model is wrong in every way that determines what the model costs, how fast it responds, and whether it returns the same answer twice. The distance between that mental model and what actually happens inside a production inference server is, I think, the most under-discussed part of the entire LLM pipeline.

I want to be specific about "brushed over," because it is a strong claim and I do not want it to read as a vibe. Training runs get papers, parameter counts, loss curves, and benchmark tables. Architecture gets diagrams. Post-training gets its own subfield. Inference gets one bullet point near the end of the model card, a logo for whatever serving framework was used, and a number for tokens per second that nobody interrogates. Meanwhile the lifetime cost of a successful model is dominated by serving it, not training it. You train once. You serve the model billions of times. Every architectural choice that looks free on a training curve has a price, and the bill arrives at inference time with interest.

This post is about why that bill is large, why the engineering that controls it is genuinely hard, and why the difficulty is invisible from the outside. None of the individual pieces are secret. They are just spread across a dozen systems papers and a handful of framework codebases, and almost nobody assembles them into a single picture of what it actually takes to turn a set of weights into a service.

## A forward pass is two different programs

The first thing that breaks the simple mental model is that autoregressive generation is not one workload. It is two workloads with opposite hardware profiles, running back to back, on the same accelerator.

The first phase is prefill. You take the entire prompt and run it through the model in parallel. Every prompt token is processed at once, which means the core operations are large matrix multiplications with high arithmetic intensity. Prefill is compute-bound. The GPU's tensor cores are the bottleneck, and they are reasonably well fed.

The second phase is decode, and it generates output one token at a time. Each token depends on every token before it, so you cannot parallelize across the sequence dimension. To produce a single new token, the server reads the entire set of model weights out of high-bandwidth memory, does a small amount of arithmetic with them, and writes one token. Then it does it again for the next token. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound. The tensor cores sit mostly idle while the memory subsystem struggles to stream weights fast enough.

The cleanest way to see how lopsided this is comes from the roofline model. For a dense model with $P$ parameters served in 16-bit precision, generating one token reads roughly $2P$ bytes of weights and performs roughly $2P$ floating point operations. If you process a batch of $B$ sequences at once, you still read the weights only once but you do the arithmetic $B$ times, so the arithmetic intensity is approximately

$$I \approx \frac{2PB}{2P} = B \quad \text{FLOPs per byte.}$$

Compare that to the hardware. An H100 has on the order of 3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth and on the order of several hundred TFLOP/s of usable 16-bit compute. The ratio of those two numbers, the ridge point of the roofline, lands somewhere in the range of 150 to 300 FLOPs per byte depending on how you count. That is the punchline that should be on the first slide of every LLM serving talk and almost never is: to push decode out of the memory-bound regime and actually use the compute you paid for, you need a batch size in the low hundreds. At batch size one, you are running the GPU at well under one percent of its arithmetic throughput. The hardware is not slow. You are just barely using it.

Everything downstream in the serving stack is a response to this single fact. The entire economic game of LLM inference is finding a way to keep the decode batch large enough to amortize the cost of reading the weights, without wrecking the latency that individual users experience.

## Batching is the whole game, and batching is adversarial

If decode at batch size one wastes the hardware, the obvious fix is to batch many users together so a single weight read serves many tokens. That is correct, and it is also where the difficulty starts, because batching autoregressive generation is nothing like batching a normal inference service.

A normal batched service collects requests, pads them to the same shape, runs one forward pass, and returns. That approach, static batching, is a disaster for LLMs. Requests arrive at different times, so the batch either waits (adding latency) or runs small (wasting compute). Requests have different prompt lengths, so padding wastes work. Worst of all, requests generate a different number of output tokens, and one sequence that wants to generate two thousand tokens holds the entire batch hostage while the sequences that finished after fifty tokens sit there doing nothing. This is head-of-line blocking, and in a static batch it can leave most of your "batched" slots generating padding.

The fix is continuous batching, also called iteration-level scheduling, introduced by Orca and now standard in every serious serving framework. Instead of treating a batch as a fixed unit that runs to completion, the scheduler makes a decision at every single decode step. A sequence that emitted its end-of-sequence token is evicted immediately and its slot is handed to a waiting request on the very next iteration. The batch is a living set that changes shape token by token. This is the difference between thirty percent and ninety percent hardware utilization in practice, and it converts the problem from a model problem into a scheduling problem. The thing deciding your throughput is not the transformer. It is a scheduler making admission and eviction decisions hundreds of times per second under a latency constraint.

That reframing matters, because scheduling is a discipline with its own deep literature, and almost none of the discourse about "running the model" acknowledges that a scheduler is running the show.

## The KV cache is an operating systems problem

Continuous batching raises an immediate question: where does the state of all those concurrent sequences live? The answer is the KV cache, and it is the second place the simple mental model collapses.

During decode, the model does not recompute attention over the whole prefix every step. It caches the key and value vectors for every previous token and reuses them. The size of that cache, in bytes, is

$$\text{KV bytes} = 2 \cdot b \cdot L \cdot n_{\text{kv}} \cdot d_h \cdot T,$$

where the leading 2 covers keys and values, $b$ is bytes per element, $L$ is the number of layers, $n_{\text{kv}}$ is the number of key/value heads, $d_h$ is the head dimension, and $T$ is the number of tokens. The thing to notice is that this grows linearly with both sequence length and the number of concurrent sequences. For a large model at long context, the KV cache for a single request can run to tens of gigabytes, and it is the KV cache, not the weights, that caps how many users you can serve at once. The weights are a fixed cost you pay once. The KV cache is a per-user, per-token tax on your remaining memory.

The naive implementation reserves a contiguous block of memory per request, sized for the maximum possible length. This is catastrophic. Most requests do not reach the maximum, so you waste enormous amounts of memory on reservations that are never used, and the memory you do free comes back in awkwardly sized holes you cannot reuse. This is internal and external fragmentation, and it is the exact problem operating systems spent decades solving for physical memory.

vLLM's PagedAttention is, explicitly, the operating system answer transplanted into a model server. The KV cache is broken into fixed-size pages. Each sequence gets a page table mapping its logical token positions to physical pages scattered anywhere in memory. Pages are allocated on demand as a sequence grows, so you never over-reserve, and freed pages go straight back into a pool with no fragmentation. The same mechanism gives you copy-on-write prefix sharing almost for free: if a thousand requests share the same system prompt, they can point at the same physical pages for that prefix instead of storing a thousand copies. Prefix caching, which most providers now expose as a billing discount, is a direct consequence of treating the cache like virtual memory.

I find this genuinely beautiful, and also a little damning. The state of the art in serving the most advanced models we have built is, at its core, a careful reimplementation of paged virtual memory from the 1960s. That is not a criticism of the work. It is an indication of how much real systems engineering is hiding under the word "deployment."

## The metrics fight each other

Once you accept that serving is a scheduling and memory problem, you have to confront the fact that there is no single number that captures "fast." There are at least three, and they are in tension.

- **Time to first token** is how long the user waits before anything appears. It is dominated by prefill and by how long the request queued before it was scheduled.
- **Time per output token**, sometimes called inter-token latency, is how fast text streams once it starts. It is dominated by decode and therefore by how large the batch is.
- **Throughput** is total tokens per second across all users on a GPU. It is the number that determines cost per token, and it improves with larger batches.

The conflict is structural. Throughput wants the biggest batch you can assemble. Per-token latency wants the smallest batch, because a bigger decode batch means each step touches more data and takes longer. You cannot maximize both. Every serving deployment is a chosen point on a latency-throughput Pareto frontier, and the right point depends on whether you are serving an interactive chat product or a bulk offline job. There is no setting that is correct in general, which means "how fast is the model" is not even a well-posed question until you specify which of these you care about and what service level you are committing to.

It gets worse when prefill and decode interfere. If a new request shows up with a very long prompt, its prefill is a big compute-heavy operation that, run naively, stalls the ongoing decode steps for every other user. The result is a periodic latency spike: everyone's stream stutters whenever a long prompt enters the system. The fixes are themselves nontrivial systems work. Chunked prefill, from the Sarathi line of work, slices a long prefill into smaller pieces and interleaves them with decode so neither starves the other. Disaggregated serving, from systems like DistServe and Splitwise, goes further and runs prefill and decode on physically separate pools of GPUs, because the two phases want different hardware and different batching, and forcing them to share one machine compromises both. We are now at the point where the optimal architecture splits a single forward pass across two clusters connected by a fast link that ships KV cache between them. That is a long way from `model.forward(x)`.

## The numerics do not do what you think

Here is the part that surprises even people who know the systems story, and the part I care about most given where my own research sits. The same model, serving the same prompt, with sampling turned off, does not reliably return the same output.

The intuition that greedy decoding at temperature zero is deterministic is wrong in production, and the reason is subtle. Floating point addition is not associative. At the bit level, $(a + b) + c$ is not equal to $a + (b + c)$. GPU kernels compute sums by splitting work across many threads and combining partial results, and the order in which those partial results get combined depends on the batch size and the kernel's tiling decisions. When your request shares a batch with three others, the reduction order inside the matrix multiplications is different than when it shares a batch with three hundred others. Different order, different rounding, occasionally a different argmax, and therefore a different token, which then changes every token after it. Thinking Machines made this argument cleanly in 2025: the dominant source of nondeterminism is not concurrency or atomics, it is the lack of batch-invariant kernels. You can fix it by writing reductions whose order does not depend on what else is in the batch, and you pay for that fix in throughput. The field's default is therefore to leave it unfixed, which means the model's output depends on server load, which is to say on how many strangers happened to be talking to it at the same instant.

Quantization compounds this. Serving at FP8 is now routine because the hardware has native support for it, INT4 weight-only quantization is common for fitting large models on smaller deployments, and even the KV cache gets quantized to squeeze in more concurrent users. Each of these changes the function being computed. The model you served at 16-bit precision and the model you served at INT4 are not the same function. They agree most of the time and disagree at the margins, and the margins are exactly where interesting behavior lives.

This is not academic to me. I work on mechanistic interpretability, and a recurring finding in my own work is that the features a sparse autoencoder identifies at full precision can shift character under aggressive quantization, particularly in layers with large outlier activations. If you are building safety monitoring or behavioral guarantees on top of features identified in a 16-bit model, and then you serve a quantized model, you are monitoring an object that is subtly not the one you studied. The interpretability literature mostly studies models as fixed mathematical functions. The serving stack quietly turns each model into a family of slightly different functions selected by deployment configuration and current load, and almost nobody draws the line connecting those two facts. I think that line is going to matter a great deal, and right now it is being brushed over inside the part of the pipeline that is already brushed over.

## Speculative decoding, and the limits of cleverness

It is worth walking through one celebrated optimization in detail, because it illustrates a pattern that runs through all of inference: every trick has a regime where it stops working, and the regime is rarely stated.

Speculative decoding attacks the sequential nature of decode. A small, cheap draft model proposes several tokens ahead. The large target model then verifies all of them in a single forward pass, accepting the longest prefix that matches what it would have produced anyway. When the draft is good, you generate several tokens for roughly the cost of one large model step. The expected number of tokens accepted per verification, for a draft of length $k$ and per-token acceptance probability $\alpha$, is

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens}] = \frac{1 - \alpha^{\,k+1}}{1 - \alpha},$$

which is why pushing $k$ higher gives diminishing returns: each additional speculative token is less likely to survive the chain of earlier acceptances. Medusa and EAGLE refine this with tree-structured drafts and lightweight heads instead of a separate draft model, and the gains in the right setting are real, often two times or better on wallclock latency.

Here is the part that gets left out. Speculative decoding trades extra compute for fewer sequential steps. It runs the target model over $k+1$ candidate positions instead of one, betting that spare compute is available. That bet is only true in the memory-bound regime, which is to say at small batch size, which is exactly the low-utilization case I described at the start. The moment you batch enough requests to make decode compute-bound, you have no spare compute to spend on speculation, and the extra verification work competes directly with real work. The speedup shrinks and can go negative. Speculative decoding and large-batch throughput are, to a real extent, in opposition. The optimization that helps a latency-sensitive low-traffic deployment is the wrong optimization for a throughput-sensitive high-traffic one. There is no universal win here, only a choice that depends on the regime, and "it depends on the regime" is precisely the kind of nuance that does not survive translation into a benchmark headline.

## Why this gets brushed over

Step back and look at what an inference server actually does. It performs admission control on incoming requests. It schedules work at fine granularity under latency constraints. It manages a paged memory cache with eviction and sharing policies. It vectorizes heterogeneous work into batches. It selects numerical kernels with throughput and stability tradeoffs. It distributes computation across machines with collective communication on the critical path. These are admission control, scheduling, virtual memory, query batching, query planning, and distributed execution. They are, almost exactly, the problems a relational database engine spent forty years solving. We are rebuilding a database engine for tensors, calling it "infra," and then acting surprised that it is hard.

I think the reason it gets brushed over is mostly a status problem dressed up as a technical one. The model is the artifact you can name and point at. It has a parameter count and a benchmark score and a press release. The serving stack has none of those. It produces no number that goes up and to the right in a way a non-specialist can appreciate. Its wins are negative wins: the latency spike that did not happen, the GPU that was not wasted, the bill that did not arrive. Negative wins are invisible, and invisible work is low status, and low-status work attracts less talent and less discussion even when it controls the economics of the entire field.

I will admit a personal bias here, because it is relevant rather than decorative. I am drawn to this layer specifically because the complexity is real and hidden, and I have an unusually low tolerance for explanations that round away the part that actually matters. "It is just a forward pass" lands on me the same way any too-clean abstraction does, as a claim that has quietly deleted the hard part to make itself feel finished. The hard part is still there. Someone is paying for it. I would rather look directly at it than agree to pretend it is simple.

## Closing

The model is the part you can see. The serving stack is the part that decides whether anyone can afford to run it, how fast it answers, and whether it answers the same way twice. We have spent a decade naming the first part in extraordinary detail and waving a hand at the second, and the hand-wave is getting expensive in proportion to how much of the world now runs on these systems.

None of this is unsolvable. Most of it is, in fact, being solved right now, in serving frameworks and systems papers that deserve far more attention than they get. I am writing this mostly to move the boundary of what counts as "the model" a little further down the stack, to where the scheduler and the pager and the numerics live. Most people see a forward pass. I see a scheduler, a memory allocator, and a numerical stability problem wearing the same coat, and I think the coat is the most interesting thing in the room.
