I threw myself off a cliff for my work. Not a metaphor for burnout. A description of what total commitment feels like from the inside: you jump, you are falling, you are building the plane on the way down, and the only reason you do not think about the ground is that the problem in front of you is more interesting than the fear.

I do not regret it. But I am starting to understand that a life is a sequence of phases, and the skill is not in maximizing any single one. It is in knowing when a phase has done its job.

## How it actually looked

I moved to Montreal from Morocco when I was seventeen to study engineering. No network, no family nearby, no safety net. Just a work ethic that bordered on compulsive and a clear sense that this was not something I could afford to waste.

So I did not waste it. I also did not do much else.

I had been this way before Montreal. I built a trading fund when I was fourteen. Not because anyone told me to. Because the problem was interesting and I did not know how to be interested in something without giving it everything. That pattern followed me to university and multiplied. Internships, engineering teams, coursework, all running in parallel, all feeling urgent. I am twenty-three. I have been running this way for most of my life.

No single commitment was unreasonable. But I never carried just one. Stacked, they left no room for anything that was not work.

I missed my brother growing up. Not in the way where you are aware of it and it hurts. In the way where you talk to him one day and realize he has a whole life you know nothing about and you cannot point to when that happened because you were not paying attention. You were debugging something. You were always debugging something.

My mom called me every week. I picked up most of the time. When I did, I was half there. She could tell. She never said anything about it. That is the kind of grace that you do not notice until much later, and when you do notice, it does not feel like grace. It feels like something you did not deserve and received anyway.

I stopped cooking. I stopped exercising with any consistency. I stopped reading things that were not about work. I stopped reaching out to friends until they stopped reaching out to me. None of this happened in a single moment. It accumulated, the way dust accumulates on a shelf you have stopped noticing. By the time you see it, the dust is the shelf.

I was not miserable. That matters, because the narrative people expect is suffering. I was not suffering. I was engaged. The work was interesting. The progress was real. The sacrifice did not feel like sacrifice in real time. It felt like focus. That is what makes it dangerous. It felt correct while it was happening.

## The sprint that became the marathon

A sprint is a valid training stimulus. Your body adapts and you come out stronger. But a sprint only works as a sprint. If you sprint for the length of a marathon, you do not get the benefits of either.

By the third and fourth year I was running on identity more than strategy. I kept going not because the pace was still optimal but because I did not know who I was without it.

There is a specific loneliness that comes from this and it is not the kind where you feel alone in a room. It is the kind where you are surrounded by people who respect your work and you realize that none of them know you. They know your output. They know your reliability. They do not know what you think about when you are not working because you are always working and you have stopped thinking about anything else. You have become very impressive and very thin. There is a lot of surface and not much underneath.

I did not recognize this while it was happening. You do not feel yourself becoming thin. You feel yourself becoming focused.

## What the cliff gave me

I want to be honest about what the intensity produced, because dismissing it would be dishonest and also because the gifts are real even when the cost was too high.

Doing quantitative work while building embedded systems while studying the math underneath both gave me a cross-domain intuition you do not develop by specializing. I can move between finance, systems engineering, and ML research without the switching cost that usually comes with crossing domains. That ability is load-bearing in everything I do now.

The intensity gave me tolerance for ambiguity. When you are twenty and building tools for people who manage real money, you learn fast that you will not understand everything before you have to ship it. That discomfort used to paralyze me. Now it is the normal operating environment.

It gave me the foundation for what I am doing now. The cliff was not pointless. The cliff was the path. I need to say that clearly because the rest of this post might sound like regret, and it is not. It is something harder to name. It is gratitude and grief at the same time, for the same thing.

## The people I looked up to

I had role models whose output I studied and whose trajectories I measured myself against. Brilliant people who had built things that mattered.

But when I looked at their lives with any honesty, I admired their work without wanting their lives. Some of them were isolated in ways they had stopped noticing. Some talked about their families with a guilt that said more than the words did. Some had achieved extraordinary things and seemed, underneath it, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

One of them, someone whose work I genuinely admired, once told me over coffee that he had not taken a real vacation in four years. He said it like it was a credential. I laughed. He did not. I realized later that he was not bragging. He was confessing, and he did not have the language to frame it as anything other than dedication.

I kept admiration and concern in separate compartments for a long time. It is a convenient arrangement. It lets you keep running toward an outcome while ignoring the full cost of arriving there.

The compartments eventually merged. I wanted their outputs but not their tradeoffs. That meant the model I was optimizing toward had the wrong objective function. That is a clean way to say it. The honest way is that I was watching people arrive at the destination I was running toward and realizing they did not look the way I expected someone to look when they got there.

## Phases

A tree puts almost all its energy into root growth early on. The visible part above ground is small, but the root system is expanding rapidly underground. Later, the allocation shifts toward vertical growth, branches, canopy. Neither phase is wrong. The mistake is staying in root-growth mode after the roots are deep enough.

I think seventeen to twenty-three was root growth. The degree, the internships, the side projects, the fund. Right phase, right time. The depth I built is the foundation for everything I am doing now.

What I am learning is that the phase ended and I did not notice. I kept putting energy underground while the rest of the tree waited.

## The decision I just made

I recently declined what most people would call a dream job. The kind of role I had been working toward for years. The kind that younger me would not have believed was on the table.


Not because the work was not interesting. It was. Not because I did not want it. I did. I wanted it in the way you want something you have been imagining for years, where the wanting has become part of your identity and saying no to it feels like saying no to yourself.

I said no because I looked at what it would require and recognized, for the first time with real clarity, that I was already at my max. Not close to it. At it. The honest version is that some mornings I was sitting in my car for a few minutes before going in, not because I was tired, but because I needed to gather something that used to be there automatically and was not there anymore.

Taking on more at my max would not produce more output. It would produce worse output, worse health, and a version of myself that I have already seen enough of to know I do not want to live there permanently. I have seen what I look like when I am running on empty and calling it discipline. It is not a person I want to become. It is a person I have already been, briefly, and I left.

Turning down something you want because you know the cost is more than you can absorb is not the same as not wanting it. It is wanting it and choosing yourself over it. I do not know if that is courage or self-preservation or both. I know it is the hardest professional decision I have made, and I know that the version of me from two years ago would not have been capable of making it.

## What I am figuring out

I do not have this resolved.

I called my mom the morning after I made the decision. I told her what I turned down. She was quiet for a long time. Then she said something in Arabic that does not translate cleanly but means, roughly, "you are finally listening." She did not mean listening to her. She meant listening to myself. She had been watching me not listen for years and she had been waiting without saying anything because she knew I would not hear it until I was ready.

That is the phone call that broke me. Not the decision. The decision was hard but it was rational. The phone call was not rational. It was my mother telling me, in the gentlest way she knew how, that she had seen everything and had been carrying the worry quietly because she loved me too much to add to the weight.

I am twenty-three and I just turned down something I wanted because I decided my own stability mattered more than my trajectory. That is not a sentence I would have been capable of writing two years ago.

The depth I built does not evaporate when I go for a walk or spend an evening cooking or call someone I have not talked to in too long. The capacity is structural. It lives in my understanding, not in my hours. The things I neglected, relationships, health, curiosity about things outside engineering, are not rewards I earn after enough achievement. They are inputs. They always were. I just could not see it from inside the freefall.

I still work hard. I still have weeks where the old pattern takes over. But I am paying attention to the parts of my life I stopped paying attention to at seventeen. And I am making decisions now that the old version of me would not have had the clarity to make.

The cliff was the right choice for the phase I was in. I jumped, I survived, I built real things on the way down. I would not change it.

But I have landed now, and the ground requires a different kind of movement than freefall. Walking is slower than falling. It is also how you get somewhere you want to stay.
