I threw myself off a cliff for my work. Not a metaphor for burnout. A description of what total commitment feels like from the inside: you jump, you are falling, you are building the plane on the way down, and the only reason you do not think about the ground is that the problem in front of you is more interesting than the fear.

I do not regret it. But I am starting to understand that a life is a sequence of phases, and the skill is not in maximizing any single one. It is in knowing when a phase has done its job.

## How it actually looked

I moved to Montreal from Morocco when I was seventeen to study engineering. No network, no family nearby, no safety net. Just a work ethic that bordered on compulsive and a clear sense that this was not something I could afford to waste.

So I did not waste it. I also did not do much else.

I had been this way before Montreal. I was fourteen the first time I disappeared into a project so completely that everything else stopped existing. That pattern followed me to university and multiplied. I kept saying yes to things. Internships, side projects, coursework, all running in parallel, all feeling urgent. I am twenty-three. I have been this way for most of my life.

No single commitment was unreasonable. But I never carried just one. Stacked, they left no room for anything that was not work.

I missed my brother growing up. Not in the way where you are aware of it and it hurts. In the way where you talk to him one day and realize he has a whole life you know nothing about and you cannot point to when that happened because you were not paying attention. 

My mom called me every week. I picked up most of the time. When I did, I was half there. She could tell. She never said anything about it. That is the kind of grace that you do not notice until much later, and when you do notice, it does not feel like grace. It feels like something you did not deserve and received anyway.

I did not stop everything. I was running a hundred kilometers a week. But that was part of it, not a counterbalance to it. The running was the same compulsion in a different container. I stopped reading things that were not about work. I stopped reaching out to friends until they stopped reaching out to me. I poured myself into two things, engineering and mileage, and let everything else go quiet. None of this happened in a single moment. It accumulated, the way dust accumulates on a shelf you have stopped noticing. By the time you see it, the dust is the shelf.

I was not miserable. That matters, because the narrative people expect is suffering. I was not suffering. I was engaged. The work was interesting. The progress was real. The sacrifice did not feel like sacrifice in real time. It felt like focus. That is what makes it dangerous. It felt correct while it was happening.

## The sprint that became the marathon

By the third and fourth year I was running on identity more than strategy. I kept going not because the pace was still optimal but because I did not know who I was without it. When your self-concept is built around being the person who never stops, slowing down does not feel like rest. It feels like disappearing.

There is a specific loneliness that comes from this and it is not the kind where you feel alone in a room. It is the kind where you are surrounded by people who respect what you do and you realize that none of them know you. They know your output. They know your reliability. They do not know what you think about when you are not working because you are always working and you have stopped thinking about anything else.

I did not recognize this while it was happening. You do not feel yourself hollowing out. You feel yourself becoming focused.

## What it cost

I want to be honest about both sides. The years of intensity taught me things I could not have learned any other way. I do not dismiss that. The foundation I built is real and I rely on it every day.

But I think I used to list those gains to myself as justification. As proof that the tradeoff was worth it. And at some point I realized I was keeping a ledger in my head, stacking skills and experiences on one side to avoid looking at what was on the other side.

The other side was not dramatic. It was not a breakdown or a crisis. It was quieter than that. It was realizing I did not have a single person in my life I could call at two in the morning who was not my mother. It was noticing that I could not remember the last time I had done something for no reason, with no goal, just because it was enjoyable. It was looking at my life and seeing something that worked very well and felt like it belonged to someone else.

## The people I looked up to

I had people whose work I studied and whose paths I measured myself against. I wanted to end up where they were.

But when I looked at their lives honestly, I admired their work without wanting their lives. Some of them were isolated in ways they had stopped noticing. Some talked about their families with a guilt that said more than the words did. Some had built things that mattered and seemed, underneath it, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

One of them once told me over coffee that he had not taken a real vacation in four years. He said it like it was a credential. I laughed. He did not. I realized later that he was not bragging. He was confessing, and he did not have the language to frame it as anything other than dedication.

I kept admiration and concern in separate compartments for a long time. Convenient, because it lets you keep running toward an outcome while ignoring the full cost of arriving there.

The compartments eventually merged. The honest version is that I was watching people arrive at the place I was running toward and realizing they did not look the way I expected someone to look when they got there.

## Phases

A tree puts almost all its energy into root growth early on. The visible part above ground is small, but the root system is expanding rapidly underground. Later, the allocation shifts. Neither phase is wrong. The mistake is staying in root-growth mode after the roots are deep enough.

I think seventeen to twenty-three was root growth. The right phase, the right time. I do not regret it.

What I am learning is that the phase ended and I did not notice. I kept putting energy underground while the rest of the tree waited.

## The decision I just made

I recently turned down a role I had been working toward for a long time. I do not want to say more about it than that, because the specifics are not the point and I do not want this to sound like I am mourning something impressive. I am mourning the version of myself that would have said yes without thinking.

I wanted it. I wanted it in the way you want something you have been imagining for years, where the wanting has become part of your identity and saying no to it feels like saying no to yourself.

I said no because I looked at what it would require and recognized, for the first time with real clarity, that I was already at my max. Not close to it. At it. Some mornings I was sitting in my car for a few minutes before going in, not because I was tired, but because I needed to gather something that used to be there automatically and was not there anymore.

Taking on more at that point would not produce better work. It would produce worse work, worse health, and a version of myself that I have already been, briefly, and do not want to go back to.

Turning down something you want because you know you cannot absorb the cost is not the same as not wanting it. It is wanting it and choosing yourself over it. I do not know if that is courage or self-preservation or both. I know it is the hardest decision I have made, and I know that the version of me from two years ago would not have been capable of making it. Not because he was stronger. Because he was not paying attention.

## What I am figuring out

I do not have this resolved.

I called my mom the right after I made my decision. I told her what I turned down. She was quiet for a long time. Then she said something in Arabic that does not translate cleanly but means, roughly, "you are finally listening." She did not mean listening to her. She meant listening to myself. She had been watching me not listen for years and she had been waiting without saying anything because she knew I would not hear it until I was ready.

That is the phone call that broke me. Not the decision. The decision was hard but it was rational. The phone call was not rational. It was my mother telling me, in the gentlest way she knew how, that she had seen everything and had been carrying the worry quietly because she loved me too much to add to the weight.

I am twenty-three and I just turned down something I wanted because I decided my own stability mattered more than my trajectory. That is not a sentence I would have been capable of writing two years ago.

The things I neglected, relationships, health, curiosity about things outside engineering, are not rewards I earn after enough achievement. They are inputs. They always were. I just could not see it from inside the freefall.

I still work hard. I still have weeks where the old pattern takes over. But I am paying attention now to the parts of my life I stopped paying attention to at seventeen. And I am making decisions that the old version of me would not have had the clarity to make.

The cliff was the right choice for the phase I was in. I jumped, I survived, I built things on the way down. I would not change it.

But I have landed now, and the ground requires a different kind of movement than freefall. Walking is slower than falling. It is also how you get somewhere you want to stay.
