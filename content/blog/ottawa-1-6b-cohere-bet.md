Ottawa’s decision to commit up to **C$1.6 billion** in equity to Cohere under the banner of a "Sovereign AI Strategy" is peak industrial policy theater. 

On paper, the narrative writes itself for politicians: back the domestic champion, keep Canadian IP within our borders, and buy a seat at the frontier AI table. 

In reality, it is a catastrophic misallocation of public capital. It misunderstands the physics of compute, misdiagnoses the bottleneck of Canadian tech, and picks a single corporate winner in a commoditizing market at peak private valuation.

Here is why this bet fails—and what \$1.6 billion should have built instead.

---

## 1. The CapEx Asymmetry: You Can't Buy a Champion on Layaway

Frontier pre-training is not a software game; it is an industrial capital war. Hyperscalers—Microsoft, Google, Meta, Amazon—are deploying **\$50B to \$100B per year** in datacenter capex and cluster buildouts. 

A \$1.6B CAD bet on a single private foundation model vendor does not purchase "sovereignty." It purchases a minority ticket on an accelerating treadmill that the Canadian government cannot afford to keep running on. 

Within 18 months, that capital will simply be chewed up by silicon depreciation, high-bandwidth interconnects, and cloud hosting bills—mostly flowing right back to US hyperscaler balance sheets (Oracle, AWS). If Cohere needs another \$5B in 2028 to train their next generation of weights, is Ottawa writing another cheque, or accepting immediate dilution?

You cannot play a global frontier arms race with municipal-scale pocket change.

---

## 2. Base Models Are Commoditizing; Equity Does Not Equal Sovereignty

Backing a proprietary base-model API vendor assumes that value in the AI stack permanently accrues to closed, generic model providers. 

The last two years have proved the opposite:
- Open-weight ecosystems (Llama, DeepSeek, Mistral) and architectural distillation are compressing the margin on generic text generation to near marginal electricity cost.
- True enterprise defensibility lives in proprietary data, domain-specialized evaluation, and test-time reasoning harnesses—not generic LLM API wrappers.
- Taking an equity stake in a company that runs its workloads on foreign-controlled cloud infrastructure does not give Canada "sovereignty." If the silicon, networking, and datacenters sit outside Canadian jurisdiction, calling the weights "sovereign" is pure marketing.

---

## 3. How to Actually Spend $1.6 Billion on Canadian AI

If the goal is to turn Canada into a compounding AI powerhouse rather than a subsidized single-tenant outpost, that capital should have been deployed into shared public infrastructure and demand-side adoption:

### A. Sovereign Compute Co-located with Canadian Clean Hydro
Canada’s true structural unfair advantage is not venture capital—it is **abundant, clean, low-cost hydroelectricity** (Quebec, BC, Manitoba, Ontario) and cold northern climates. 

Take that \$1.6 billion and construct open, publicly governed GPU clusters dedicated to Canadian universities (Mila, Vector, Amii), research labs, and early-stage startups. Sovereign compute is a public utility, like roads or power grids. Providing guaranteed, low-cost FLOPs to thousands of domestic builders creates durable ecosystem leverage; buying shares in a single cap table does not.

### B. Subsidize the Buyers, Not One Seller
Instead of handing billions to one vendor, establish a federal **AI Compute & Deployment Voucher** program for the domestic economy. 

Subsidize the integration of applied AI across Canadian healthcare, advanced manufacturing, natural resources, transport, and finance. Distributing \$1.6B in adoption vouchers across 500+ Canadian startups and traditional industries forces model providers to compete on performance and price, while building real operational muscle in the Canadian GDP.

### C. Stem the Brain Drain (Talent Retention Over Corporate Subsidies)
Canada trained the intellectual architects of modern deep learning, yet we lose our best PhDs and engineers to Silicon Valley and New York every year. Why? Wage compression, high personal tax burdens, and a lack of local experimental compute.

A fraction of \$1.6 billion could fund non-dilutive engineering fellowships, aggressive research grants, and high-performance compute stipends that actually keep our top technical builders in Montreal, Toronto, and Vancouver.

---

## The Takeaway

You do not build a resilient national technology ecosystem by crowning a single corporate champion at the top of a valuation cycle. 

Governments should fund the commodity foundations—cheap clean power, open compute access, and elite talent retention—and let open competition build the rest. C\$1.6 billion could have electrified Canada's computational spine. Instead, we bought an expensive hedge on a single corporate horse.
