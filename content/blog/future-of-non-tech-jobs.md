Most commentary on AI and non-tech employment follows a predictable script. Either AI will destroy all the jobs, or AI will create new ones we cannot imagine yet, or the answer is somewhere in the middle and everyone should learn prompt engineering. These takes are not wrong so much as they are uninteresting. They describe the surface of the water without saying anything about the currents underneath.

I want to talk about five structural forces that I think are actually reshaping non-tech work. None of them map cleanly onto the "will my job be automated?" framing, because that framing is itself the problem. Automation is not a binary. It is a set of pressures that deform the shape of work in ways that are more specific, more strange, and more consequential than "replaced" or "safe."

## The Legibility Trap

The default model for predicting which jobs AI will affect sorts them into "routine" versus "creative," or "manual" versus "cognitive." This is the wrong axis. The axis that actually matters is *legibility*: how completely can the work be formally described?

Machine learning needs specification. A model can only learn a task if that task can be represented as a mapping from inputs to outputs, with enough examples to learn the mapping. The more legible a job is, the easier it is to generate training data for it, to define evaluation metrics for it, and to build a system that does it.

A tax preparer's workflow is almost perfectly legible. Take these forms, apply these rules, produce this output. The fact that it requires expertise does not make it illegible. It just means the rulebook is thick. A bookkeeper's workflow is legible. An insurance underwriter's workflow is legible. These are jobs where a sufficiently motivated team could write a complete specification of what the job entails, and a model could be trained on the spec.

Contrast this with a social worker assessing whether a child is safe in a home. The inputs are a conversation, a living room, a parent's body language, a child's silence. The output is a judgment call with life-altering consequences. There is no spec. There is no rubric that captures what an experienced social worker notices when something feels wrong. The knowledge is *illegible*, not because it is unimportant, but because it resists formalization. The practitioner often cannot articulate it themselves.

This reframes the entire automation conversation. The question is not "is this job creative?" It is "can this job be written down?" A surprising number of white-collar jobs that feel sophisticated are actually highly legible. And a surprising number of blue-collar jobs that feel simple are deeply illegible. A plumber diagnosing a strange noise in the pipes of a 1940s building is doing something closer to medical differential diagnosis than to following a manual. The knowledge is embodied, contextual, and resistant to formalization.

The top-left, low legibility and high liability, is where humans are most durable. But the interesting quadrants are the other two, which I will return to.

## Credential Compression

AI does not eliminate jobs. It eliminates the *premium* on credentials.

Consider the structure of a law firm. At the bottom, paralegals do document review, legal research, and first-draft work. Above them, junior associates do more complex analysis and client-facing work. Above them, senior partners do strategy, negotiation, and relationship management. Each tier requires more credentialing: a certificate, then a JD, then years of practice and partnership. The credential ladder is eight to twelve years long and each rung costs real money.

AI compresses this ladder. A paralegal with access to competent AI tools can produce work that previously required a junior associate. A junior associate with AI can handle matters that previously required a senior one. The work does not disappear. The credential gatekeeping that determined who was allowed to do it becomes economically irrational.

Credential Compression

This is not a thought experiment. It is already happening in radiology, where AI-assisted technicians can flag anomalies that previously required a radiologist's eye. It is happening in accounting, where bookkeepers with AI tools produce analyses that previously required a CPA. The pattern is consistent: the middle tiers of professional hierarchies get compressed into the bottom tier, augmented by AI.

The interesting question is what happens to the guilds. The American Bar Association, medical licensing boards, CPA certification bodies, these institutions exist to gatekeep entry into professions. Their economic justification is that the credential ensures competence. But if a less-credentialed person plus AI can produce equivalent work, the credential is no longer a guarantee of superior output. It is a toll booth on a road that now has a bypass.

I do not think the guilds disappear. They will fight, and they have regulatory capture on their side. But the pressure is real, and it will produce strange outcomes. Expect to see credentialing bodies redefine their purpose from "ensuring competence" to "ensuring accountability," which brings me to the next point.

## Accountability as a Service

Here is a fact about AI that most people acknowledge but few follow to its conclusion: AI cannot be sued.

If an AI system generates a misdiagnosis, writes a contract with a fatal error, or produces a financial plan that loses a client's retirement savings, there is no entity to hold legally responsible in the way our legal system requires. You cannot depose a language model. You cannot revoke its license. You cannot send it to prison for malpractice.

This means that every domain involving legal liability, medicine, law, engineering, financial advice, construction, will continue to require a human in the loop. But the human's role changes. The AI generates the diagnosis, the draft, the plan. The human reviews it, signs it, and accepts legal responsibility for it. The human's primary value is no longer their expertise. It is their *suability*.

The Accountability Gap

This is not a cynical framing. It is a description of how liability law works. Someone must be the named defendant. Someone must carry malpractice insurance. Someone must have a license that can be revoked. AI cannot fill any of these roles, regardless of how competent it becomes. The result is a new category of professional whose core function is to serve as a liability sink for AI-generated decisions.

Look at the top-right quadrant of the matrix above: high legibility, high liability. Pharmacists, building inspectors, tax attorneys, commercial pilots. These are jobs where AI can do most of the cognitive work, but a human must still sign off because the consequences of errors are too severe to leave unaccounted for. The human becomes a regulatory requirement more than a functional one.

This will feel deeply unsatisfying to the people in those roles. Being paid to accept liability rather than to exercise judgment is a different kind of work, and not obviously a better one. But it is stable, because the legal system moves slowly, and "accountability as a service" is not a problem that AI can solve for itself.

## The Taste Economy

When the cost of producing something drops to near zero, the scarce resource becomes the ability to judge what is good.

This has already happened in music. Recording and production tools are effectively free. Anyone with a laptop can produce a track that sounds professional. The result is not that musicians disappeared. The result is that *curation* became the bottleneck. Playlist editors, A&R scouts, algorithm designers who surface the right song at the right moment, these are the roles that captured the value. The ability to produce became common. The ability to select became rare.

The same dynamic is spreading to every field where AI collapses production costs. Legal drafting. Marketing copy. Architectural rendering. Interior design proposals. Financial models. In each case, AI can produce a first draft that is adequate. Often multiple drafts, in seconds. The person who can look at five AI-generated options and pick the right one, or recognize that all five are wrong and articulate *why*, becomes the valuable one.

This is not the same as "soft skills" or "creativity," the usual refuge of optimistic AI commentary. Taste is specific and domain-dependent. A litigation partner's ability to sense that a brief's argument will not land with a particular judge is a form of taste that requires decades of courtroom experience. An experienced chef's ability to know that a dish needs acid, not salt, is taste. A building contractor's ability to look at plans and know which subcontractor will cut corners on that particular detail is taste.

The common thread is that taste requires enough expertise to recognize quality, combined with enough experience to have calibrated intuitions about what works in context. AI can produce. It cannot yet taste. And taste, by its nature, is illegible, which brings us back to the first point.

## The Hard Problem Residual

Here is the least intuitive consequence of partial automation: it makes the remaining human work harder.

Consider accounting. Today, an accountant's workload is a mix of routine (categorizing transactions, reconciling accounts, preparing standard filings) and non-routine (handling ambiguous tax situations, advising on entity structure, navigating audits). Maybe 70% routine, 30% edge cases. The routine work is what AI will absorb first, because it is legible.

What remains is the 30%. But now that 30% is 100% of the human accountant's job. Every task that lands on their desk is, by definition, something that AI could not handle. Every client interaction is a hard problem. There is no easy work to break up the day, no routine to provide rhythm. The job becomes cognitively denser, more demanding, and more stressful, even as the profession as a whole shrinks.

This is the hard problem residual. When you automate the easy parts of a job, you do not just reduce the number of workers needed. You change the *nature* of the remaining work. The humans who stay in the profession face a job that is 100% edge cases, all day, every day. This has implications for training, burnout, compensation, and professional identity that I do not think anyone is seriously grappling with.

The medical analogy is illustrative. If AI handles routine diagnostics, the remaining human physicians are essentially specialists in diagnostic uncertainty. Every patient they see is one where the AI said "I do not know." This is exhausting work. It is also high-stakes work, because the cases that stump AI are often the ones where the stakes are highest. The job pays more. The job is also worse.

## What I Am Not Saying

I am not saying non-tech jobs will be fine. Some will not be. The bottom-right quadrant of the legibility-liability matrix is a genuine dead zone for human employment, and a lot of people work there.

I am also not saying the answer is to learn to code or learn prompt engineering or any other version of "upskill into tech." That advice assumes the tech sector itself is stable, which is a bet I would not take right now.

What I am saying is that the forces reshaping non-tech work are more specific and more structural than the standard commentary suggests. Legibility, not routine, determines vulnerability. Credentials are compressing, not disappearing. Liability creates a floor under human involvement that has nothing to do with competence. Taste becomes the scarce resource when production is free. And the humans who remain in partially automated professions face a harder job, not an easier one.

These are not comfortable conclusions. But they are, I think, more honest than "learn to adapt" or "soft skills will save you." The future of non-tech work is not a single story. It is five different stories happening simultaneously, and most of the commentary is only telling one of them.
