It is not often that I get to review a paper written by someone I know personally. In fact this might be the first time. Maxwell Forbes is a coauthor on this one, and there is something slightly surreal about reading a paper with the same adversarial scrutiny I bring to everything in this series, and then remembering that I could just text Max with questions. I will try not to let that soften the analysis. Reviewing a friend's work comes with an obligation to be more honest, not less.

"The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration" (Holtzman, Buys, Du, Forbes, Choi, 2020) addresses a problem that everyone who has worked with language models has encountered: why does text generated by maximization-based decoding sound so flat, so repetitive, so obviously machine-generated? The answer the paper proposes is nucleus sampling, also known as top-p sampling. It has since become the default decoding strategy for virtually every deployed language model. The paper is one of those contributions that became so standard so quickly that people forget someone had to write it.

## The Problem: Maximization Is Not How Language Works

Language models assign probabilities to sequences of tokens. The "obvious" way to generate text is to find the highest-probability sequence. Beam search approximates this. Greedy decoding is the degenerate case. And the result is almost never what a human would write. It loops, it repeats, it is bland. Holtzman et al. call this "degeneration" and document it extensively.

The key insight is that human language is not a maximization process. Humans do not produce the single most likely next word at every position. They produce text that is *probable enough* while maintaining variety and surprise. Natural text lives in a region of probability space that is high-probability but not maximum-probability. Beam search collapses into a degenerate region that real language avoids.

The paper makes this point quantitatively. Beam search text has *higher* likelihood than human text under the model. It is "more probable" and it is worse. Probability and quality are not the same thing.

## Nucleus Sampling

The fix is straightforward. At each decoding step, take the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability mass exceeds a threshold $p$, then sample from that set with renormalized probabilities.

$$\sum_{x \in V^{(p)}} P(x \mid x_{1:t-1}) \geq p$$

The critical property is that the nucleus size adapts to the model's confidence. When the model is confident, the nucleus is small and sampling is constrained. When the model is uncertain, the nucleus is large and sampling explores broadly. The randomness is calibrated to the model's own uncertainty, not fixed by a hyperparameter.

Compare this to top-k sampling, where you always sample from the top $k$ tokens regardless of how the mass is distributed. If $k=40$ and the model puts 95% on a single token, top-k wastes effort on 39 near-impossible tokens. If the mass is spread across 200 plausible continuations, top-k cuts off 160 of them arbitrarily. Top-p avoids both failure modes by letting the distribution itself determine the cutoff. It is elegant not because it is mathematically deep, but because it correctly identifies the problem and proposes the natural fix.

## What the Paper Gets Right

The diagnostic section is stronger than the prescriptive section, and I mean that as a compliment. The authors spend significant effort establishing *why* maximization fails, with quantitative analysis and carefully chosen examples. One analysis I find particularly effective is their measurement of how per-token probability changes as generated sequences get longer. In beam search, confidence *increases* with length. The model collapses into a self-reinforcing loop: each high-probability token makes the next high-probability token more likely, and the sequence converges on a repetitive attractor. In human text, uncertainty fluctuates but does not systematically shrink.

## Where I Push Back

The paper frames the problem as one of decoding strategy and solves it at the decoding level. This is pragmatically correct. But it obscures a deeper question: why does the model assign high probability to degenerate text in the first place?

If a language model were a perfect model of human language, the maximum-probability sequence would be a plausible human utterance. The fact that it is not tells us something about the gap between the training objective (minimize cross-entropy) and the desired behavior (produce text that sounds human). Nucleus sampling papers over this misalignment by adding calibrated randomness. It works, but it does not address the underlying issue.

The paper also predates instruction-tuned and RLHF-trained models. The degeneration problem is less severe in modern chat models, because RLHF reshapes the output distribution to be less spiky and more aligned with human preferences. Some of the paper's most dramatic examples of degeneration would not occur in a modern fine-tuned model. The paper solved the right problem at the right time. The problem has since partially dissolved, not because nucleus sampling fixed it, but because the training process changed.

One more thing the paper does not fully resolve: the interaction between nucleus sampling and temperature scaling. In practice, deployed systems use both together. There is no principled theory for choosing $p$ and $T$ jointly. They are tuned by vibes, which is somewhat ironic for a paper that advocates for principled decoding.

## Why This Paper Still Matters

The deeper insight, that natural language occupies a region of probability space that is not the mode, connects to broader questions about what language models actually learn. Minimizing cross-entropy is not the same as maximizing quality. The gap between these two objectives is where most of the interesting problems in language generation live.

It is a strange experience, reading a friend's paper with the same adversarial lens I apply to work by strangers. I found things to push back on, as I always do. I also found a paper that identified a real problem, proposed the right solution, and changed how the entire field generates text. Not many papers clear that bar.
