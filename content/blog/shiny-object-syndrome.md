I have a folder of repositories that all stop in roughly the same place. Around sixty percent finished. Far enough in that the interesting problem is solved, not far enough that anyone other than me could run the thing. For a long time I described this to myself as breadth, or curiosity, or a healthy appetite for ideas. It was none of those. It was shiny object syndrome, and the reason it took me years to name it honestly is that it does not feel like a failure from the inside. It feels like enthusiasm.

I want to argue against the usual framing, which is that this is a discipline problem fixable with more willpower and a sterner inner voice. It is not. It is a measurement problem. The reward you feel when you start something new is real, mechanical, and almost completely decoupled from whether the new thing is worth doing. If you run on that reward without correcting for it, you will reliably spend your best energy on the part of every project that matters least.

## The reward fires at the wrong time

The pleasure of a project is front-loaded. The beginning is the best part by a wide margin. The problem is fresh, the design in your head is still perfect because no contact with reality has dented it yet, and every decision is still open. There is no legacy code, no accumulated compromise, no boring remainder. You are naming things and sketching architecture and feeling the specific high of pure possibility. None of that high is earned by shipping anything. It is paid out in full on day one.

The value is back-loaded. It lives almost entirely in the last stretch, the part that turns a clever prototype into a thing that works for someone who is not you. Error handling. The edge cases you only found by using it. Documentation. Making it installable. The unglamorous final forty percent where the actual usefulness is created. That stretch produces nearly all of the value and nearly none of the fun.

The reward curve and the value curve are therefore close to inverted. The most rewarding moment of a project produces the least value, and the most valuable stretch produces the least reward. Shiny object syndrome is what happens when you optimize honestly for the reward. You keep returning to the one move that reliably feels good, which is starting, and you keep avoiding the one that does not, which is finishing. The behavior is not irrational. It is rational with respect to the wrong objective.

## The comparison is always rigged

Every time I have been tempted to drop what I was building for something newer, I was comparing two things that cannot be fairly compared. I knew every flaw of the current project, because I was inside it, in the messy middle, staring at the compromises I had already made. I knew only the promise of the new one, because the new one was still an idea, or a demo, or a README. I was weighing the fully illuminated problems of the thing I understood against the marketing of the thing I did not.

That comparison is structurally unfair, and it always favors the new thing. It has to. One side is showing you its worst and most detailed truth, and the other is showing you a brochure.

The trap is that switching does not escape this. The new project becomes the current project. A few weeks in, it shows you its own messy middle, its own compromises, its own boring remainder, and right on cue some third thing appears on the horizon and compares favorably for exactly the same rigged reason. Shiny object syndrome is not a string of reasonable local decisions that happen to add up badly. It is one broken comparison run over and over, because the thing driving it is broken in a fixed direction.

The same bias operates on tools, not just on your own work. The framework being announced this week is in its best-marketed, least-tested state. Its failure modes are undiscovered because nobody has run it in anger yet. The tool you already use has visible, known, bounded problems, which is precisely why it looks worse. When you trade the thing you understand for the thing you do not, you are trading known and bounded problems for unknown and unbounded ones, and you are doing it because the unknown ones are not visible yet. They will be.

## Boredom wearing the costume of judgment

None of this means you should finish everything you start. Some projects genuinely deserve to die. The honest difficulty is that from the inside, "this is truly not worth finishing" and "this has reached the boring hard part" feel identical. Both present as a sudden loss of interest and a sudden attractiveness of alternatives. Telling them apart is the entire skill.

I use two tests. The first is to imagine the project actually finished, shipped, and working, and ask whether I still want it. If a completed version genuinely does not appeal to me, that is a real kill signal, and I should stop now and save the remaining effort. If imagining it done makes me want it again, and only the remaining work repels me, then I am not judging the project. I am bored, and the boredom is wearing the costume of judgment.

The second test is whether I can state the reason for quitting in one sentence. A real dead end usually has a crisp cause. The core assumption turned out to be false. The thing it would compete with is already better and free. The data I needed does not exist. Boredom cannot produce a sentence like that. It produces a vague sense that my energy has moved somewhere else, and the somewhere else is always conveniently new. When I cannot name the reason, the reason is almost always that there is no reason, only the pull.

## What I actually do

The system I have settled on is mostly a set of mechanisms for making the rigged comparison honest and the kill-or-continue decision explicit.

I capture instead of acting. Every new idea goes onto a list, and the rule is that I am not allowed to start it for two weeks. Most shiny objects do not survive being written down and ignored for two weeks. The shine was the whole appeal, and it wears off on paper faster than it does in my head. The list also does quieter work. A large part of what makes a new idea feel urgent is the fear of losing it, and writing it down removes that fear, which removes most of the urgency with it.

I run one in, one out. I do not get to start a project while two are already open and unfinished. To begin a third, I have to either finish one or formally kill one. Nothing is allowed to sit in silent limbo, which is where sixty-percent projects go to avoid being judged. A killed project gets a one-line obituary stating why it died. That single sentence forces the boredom-versus-judgment distinction to actually be made rather than quietly avoided.

I write down the reason before I commit. Before a project graduates from the list, I write what it is for and what "done" means in concrete terms. The point is to leave a note from the version of me who was thinking clearly to the version who will later be bored and looking for an exit. When bored me wants to bail, bored me has to argue with that note. Often the note wins, because it predicted this exact moment and labeled it the boring hard part rather than a real problem.

The last principle is the simplest and the one I resisted longest. I optimize for finishing one thing over starting three. A single completed project teaches things that no quantity of abandoned ones can, because the lessons live in the last forty percent that I keep skipping. Finishing is its own skill, separate from building, and it is trained only by finishing.

## What it actually costs

The cost of shiny object syndrome is not the wasted hours. Hours are recoverable, and exploration that goes nowhere is a normal part of doing real work. The cost is that nothing compounds.

A finished project is an asset that keeps paying. It teaches you the part of the craft that only exists at the end. It becomes something you can build the next thing on top of. It is evidence you can point at. A project left at sixty percent is none of that. It is a sunk cost with a tidy commit history. Ten finished things compound into genuine expertise and a body of work that exists in the world. Ten things left at sixty percent compound into nothing, just a folder and the lingering sense of having been busy.

I will add that being wired for deep focus does not save you from this. My own profile, which is on the autism spectrum, gives me a real defense. I can stay with a structurally deep problem long past the point where most people switch, and that is genuinely useful. But the novelty pull runs on a different channel from focus. It is perfectly happy to take my capacity for depth and aim it at a new deep thing. The wiring helps me go deep. It does nothing, on its own, to keep me going deep on one thing rather than serially restarting.

## Closing

The newest idea will always look like the best one, because it is the only one I have not yet had to actually build. It carries no scars, because it has not met reality. That is not a reason to trust it. It is the precise reason to distrust it.

The shine is real. It has just never once told me the truth about what was worth my time.
