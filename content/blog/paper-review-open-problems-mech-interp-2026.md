I do not normally review survey papers in this series. Survey papers age badly. They optimize for completeness, which is the opposite of what makes a paper memorable, and by the time most readers reach a survey, the survey is already partly out of date.

I am making an exception for "Open Problems in Mechanistic Interpretability" (Sharkey, Chughtai, Batson, Lindsey, Wu, Bushnaq, Heimersheim, Ortega, Bloom, Biderman, Conmy, Nanda, Geiger, Marx, and others, January 2025). I am making it for two reasons. The first is that the paper is unusually honest, in a field that has a particular weakness for over-claiming. The second is that it was published at exactly the right inflection point. Late 2024 SAE work had just hit its scale ceiling on Claude 3 Sonnet and Gemma. The Anthropic attribution-graphs papers were six weeks away from publication. The Gemma Scope dictionary releases were in their first wave of community adoption. The paper captured the field's self-image at the moment just before the next wave broke.

Reading it from June 2026, with eighteen months of hindsight, is more useful than reading it cold. We now know which of its open problems got partially solved, which got worse, and which the field quietly stopped caring about. The state of mechanistic interpretability in 2026 is the difference between what Sharkey et al. flagged as open and what is open today.

This post is a paper review and a state-of-the-field assessment in the same document. I do not think you can do one without the other anymore.

## What the Paper Actually Is

The structure of "Open Problems in Mechanistic Interpretability" is best understood as a coalition document rather than a research paper. The author list is forty-plus people from Anthropic, Google DeepMind, EleutherAI, Apollo Research, MIT, Stanford, Cambridge, and several independent groups. The contribution is not a result. The contribution is a taxonomy.

The taxonomy organizes interpretability into three layers.

**Methods.** How do you decompose a model into interpretable components? This is the largest section and contains four sub-areas: reverse engineering (recovering circuits from weights), concept-based methods (probing for human-defined concepts), developmental interpretability (studying the formation of structure during training), and interventional methods (causal patching, ablation, steering).

**Applications.** What is interpretability *for*? The paper distinguishes between safety applications (deception detection, alignment auditing, monitoring of dangerous capabilities), capability applications (debugging, model editing, performance prediction), and scientific applications (understanding what neural networks compute as a class of objects).

**Foundations.** What are the conceptual assumptions the field is operating under, and which of them might be wrong? This is the smallest section but the most important. It surfaces the linear representation hypothesis, the feature decomposition assumption, the modularity assumption, and the assumption that the right primitives for understanding a transformer are the ones that make sense to humans.

For each open problem in each layer, the paper provides a short statement, the current state of work on it as of late 2024, and a list of what would constitute progress. Some problems get a paragraph. Some get a page. The asymmetry tracks roughly with how active each sub-area is.

I want to be explicit that this is an unusual kind of paper. It is not making a claim. It is enumerating what the field's leadership thinks is unfinished. The value is in the enumeration, not in any single sub-claim, and reviewing it eighteen months later is the natural way to evaluate it.

## What Got Solved (Or At Least Substantially Advanced)

Several open problems that Sharkey et al. flagged in late 2024 are no longer open in mid-2026. Or rather: they are open in the sense that no problem of any depth is ever fully closed, but they have moved from "blocking" to "tractable."

**Multi-layer feature decomposition.** The paper notes that SAEs trained at individual layers do not naturally compose, and that understanding how features at one layer relate to features at another is an open problem. This was true in late 2024. It is substantially less true now. The work on cross-layer transcoders (Templeton, Conerly, Marks, et al., in the Anthropic Tracing the Thoughts companion piece, March 2025) gave the field a method that decomposes layer-to-layer transitions rather than layer activations, and recovers the implicit MLP-mediated computation between layers as a sparse set of feature-to-feature mappings. The Lindsey et al. attribution-graph work then used these transcoders to draw end-to-end circuit diagrams in Claude 3.5 Haiku that span twelve to twenty layers and are mostly readable.

I would not say this problem is solved. The transcoders are still slow to train, they still leave a substantial reconstruction error budget, and the resulting attribution graphs are noisy at the long-tail edges. But "no method exists for cross-layer feature composition" is not the right framing in 2026. The right framing is "the methods that exist work, but expensively, and only on small models with substantial human curation."

**Automated feature labeling at scale.** The paper flags labeling as a bottleneck and references Bills et al. (2023) as the prior art. Eighteen months later, automated labeling is no longer the bottleneck. Goodfire and Neuronpedia both ship pipelines that label SAE features with frontier models at industrial throughput, and the labels correlate with human judgment well enough that the labeling step is not where mistakes accumulate. The bottleneck has moved upstream: the labels are produced too easily, and the field has not built corresponding infrastructure for verifying them. This is the gap I am building my [thesis methodology](pflioblog/blog/automated-causal-validation-sae-features) into. I think Sharkey et al. correctly diagnosed labeling as a bottleneck and underestimated how quickly the labeling step itself would be automated relative to verification.

**Specific high-impact circuits.** The paper enumerates a list of model behaviors that the field had not yet reverse-engineered, and asks for circuit-level explanations. Several of these have been delivered. The IOI circuit (Wang et al. 2023) and greater-than circuit (Hanna et al. 2023) were in place by late 2024. In 2025, we got the medical-diagnosis circuit (Lindsey et al. 2025, in the Biology paper), the multi-hop fact-retrieval circuit, the planning-on-rhyming-poems circuit, and the refusal direction analysis (Arditi et al. 2024) that turned out to generalize across many open-weight models. The "we have only handful of circuits" framing of late 2024 has been replaced by "we have several dozen, and the methodology for finding them is reproducible." That is genuine progress, even if each individual circuit is still much smaller than the actual computation.

**Linear representation hypothesis, qualified.** The paper notes that the linear representation hypothesis is a working assumption with mixed evidence, and lists understanding when it holds and when it fails as an open problem. The 2025 work on multi-dimensional features (Engels et al., Bushnaq et al.) clarified the picture substantially. Some concepts are linear-and-monosemantic, some are linear-and-polysemantic-in-a-few-directions, some are organized in low-dimensional manifolds (days of the week, months, calendar dates) where the right primitive is a circle or a torus rather than a single direction, and some appear to be non-linear in ways the field does not yet have good tools for. The hypothesis was not refuted, but it was significantly refined. The picture in 2026 is "linear representation, with caveats that we now know how to test for."

These are the most clear-cut wins. There are several smaller advances I will not enumerate: better SAE training (matryoshka, end-to-end, transcoders), better intervention tooling (nnsight, TransformerLens 2.0), and the publication of Gemma Scope which made multi-layer SAE work accessible to anyone with one GPU and a weekend. The field moved.

## What Got Worse

Some of the open problems the paper flagged got harder, not easier, in the eighteen months after publication. I want to be specific about which ones, because I see a tendency to treat the entire field as monotonically improving, and that is not what is happening.

**Faithfulness of feature labels.** The paper explicitly flags faithfulness as an open problem and treats it as a methodological gap. The gap has widened. The reason is asymmetric speed: labeling pipelines got dramatically better in 2025, and verification pipelines did not. The field went from a few thousand labeled features per model to a few million labeled features per model, and the verification rate did not scale with the labeling rate. By mid-2026, most published feature labels are not causally validated, and the labels that are validated tend to live in narrow, well-studied circuits where the validation came incidentally rather than by design.

This is a particular instance of a more general pattern. When the bottleneck in a research pipeline gets removed, the field rushes through the bottleneck and produces a much larger pile of unverified results. The pile then has to be sorted at a later stage, and the sorting infrastructure rarely keeps pace. Sharkey et al. predicted this in passing. They underestimated how dramatic the asymmetry would be.

**Generalization across models.** The paper flags transferability of interpretability results as an open problem: do features found in one model show up in another, do circuits generalize, can interventions developed for model A be ported to model B. The 2025 evidence on this question is messy and mostly negative. The Anthropic crosscoders work (Lindsey, Templeton, et al., October 2024) showed that you can find features that are stable across versions of the same model family, but transferring across architectures (Claude to Gemma to Llama) is much harder, and several papers in 2025 have reported that features named the same way in different SAEs do not produce the same downstream behavior under intervention.

The field's working assumption in 2024 was that interpretability findings would generalize across architectures because the linear representation hypothesis is architecture-agnostic. The 2025-2026 evidence is more pessimistic. Architecture matters, training data matters, the specific scale of the model matters, and a feature labeled "deception" in one model is not necessarily the deception feature you would want to monitor in another. Transferability is a harder problem in 2026 than the field thought it was in 2024.

**MLP interpretability in non-toy models.** The paper notes that MLPs remain less interpretable than attention, and lists this as an open problem. Attention interpretation has continued to mature (we have the Mathematical Framework, we have circuit analyses that draw on QK and OV decomposition, we have head ablation tooling that works). MLP interpretation, despite the SAE wave, is still much weaker. We can decompose MLP activations into sparse features. We can label those features. We have much less idea of what the *MLP layer as a computation* is doing relative to its inputs. The transcoder work helps, but transcoders are themselves a substitute for the MLP rather than a description of it. The MLP-as-key-value-store framing (Geva et al. 2021) has been refined but not replaced. In 2026, "what is the MLP layer doing in a way I can explain to someone who has not read four papers" is still a question I cannot answer crisply.

**Compute-efficient interpretability.** The paper acknowledges that interpretability methods are computationally expensive and lists efficiency improvements as an open problem. The cost has not come down meaningfully. Training a high-quality SAE on a frontier model is still a multi-day GPU job. Running a thorough activation patching study on a single circuit is still a week of compute. The total cost of a circuit-level analysis of a single behavior in a single model is probably between fifty thousand and a quarter million dollars depending on rigor. Some of the new tooling (transcoders, attribution graphs) is *more* expensive than what it replaces, not less. The field has not figured out how to make interpretability cheap, and there is no clear roadmap to making it cheap. This is a serious problem because cheap is what enables interpretability to be deployed continuously rather than as a research artifact.

## What the Field Quietly Stopped Caring About

This is the section I wanted to write the most, because it is the part of a state-of-field review that almost never gets written. There are open problems Sharkey et al. listed in 2024 that the field has, in 2026, simply stopped working on. Not because they were solved. Because the working consensus drifted, the people who cared moved to other problems, or the methodological assumptions that made the question well-posed got replaced.

**Probing methods as a research direction.** The paper allocates substantial space to concept-based probing (linear probes, supervised probing, activation similarity). In 2024, this was a live methodological alternative to SAE-style decomposition. In 2026, probing has been quietly absorbed into a supporting role. People still use linear probes as a sanity check on SAE features. Nobody, as far as I can tell, is doing primary research on probe methodology. The Conmy et al. 2024 paper showing that probes can recover features that SAEs miss was the last major probing-methodology paper I can name, and it generated less follow-up than I would have expected. This is not because the methodology was wrong. It is because the field has converged on intervention as the primary signal and treats probing as a measurement tool rather than a research target.

**Distributed representations, plural.** The paper cites the long-running tension between localist and distributed views of neural representation, and lists open questions about distributed encoding. By 2026 this debate has dissolved without anyone declaring victory. The working consensus is that representations are distributed, the distribution is sparse-once-decomposed-correctly, and the right object of study is the SAE feature rather than the neuron or the population. Whether this is "the localist view won" or "the distributed view won" depends on how you squint, but either way the active research has moved on. I do not know of a single paper from 2025 or 2026 that engages the localist-distributed debate as a primary subject.

**Symbolic-style explanation extraction.** The paper devotes a section to the question of whether neural networks can be approximated by symbolic programs and whether such approximations preserve the relevant computational structure. This was a live research direction in 2023 and 2024 (the program-extraction line of work). It is essentially dead in 2026. Not because anyone proved it impossible, but because the empirical results in 2025 (small program extractions that capture less than the SAE-based decomposition explains, and that do not generalize to held-out inputs) shifted the field's intuition. Symbolic extraction is occasionally still mentioned in the context of mathematical-reasoning circuits, but as a primary research methodology it has been dropped.

**Causal abstractions.** The paper discusses the causal-abstraction framework (Geiger et al.) as one of the methodological contenders for grounding interpretability claims. In 2026 this framework is still cited and still used, but as a formalization for results obtained by other methods rather than as a research method in its own right. The expected research line, where you specify the abstraction first and verify it against the model second, has not produced many 2025 papers. The actual practice, in 2026, is to do SAE-style decomposition first, run causal interventions to get measurements, and then occasionally cite causal abstraction theory as a framework for what the measurements mean. The framework survived. The methodology that was supposed to come with it did not.

I want to be careful here. "The field stopped caring" is not the same as "the question is uninteresting" or "the question was answered." It often means "the practical infrastructure went elsewhere and the original question lost the people working on it." Some of these directions may come back. But for a state-of-2026 read, they are not where the energy is.

## The Three Things That Define 2026

If you ask me what the actual shape of mechanistic interpretability in mid-2026 is, here are the three things I would point to. None of them are perfectly framed in Sharkey et al., though all are present in some form.

**Attribution graphs are the dominant artifact.** The Lindsey et al. work on Claude 3.5 Haiku in March 2025, and the methodological refinements that followed in summer and fall, established attribution graphs as the standard output of a circuit-level interpretability study. An attribution graph is a directed graph where nodes are features (typically transcoder features at specific layers) and edges are linear influences computed from the model weights. The graph is constructed for a specific input or input distribution, traced backward from the output, and pruned to retain only the edges that account for some threshold fraction of the output behavior. This is now what a "circuit" looks like in publication: not a hand-drawn diagram with named heads, but a computer-generated graph with several hundred nodes and several thousand edges, summarized by feature labels.

This shifted the field in three ways. It shifted the unit of analysis from the head or MLP to the feature. It shifted the methodology from human-curated circuit drawing to automated graph construction. And it shifted the standard of evidence from "this head is doing this" (a behavioral claim about a component) to "this graph accounts for this behavior" (a quantitative claim about a structure). Sharkey et al. anticipated this shift but did not predict the speed.

**SAE features are infrastructure, not research output.** In 2024, training an SAE was a research contribution. By mid-2026, an SAE is a piece of infrastructure that you train as a precondition to doing interpretability rather than as the result of doing it. Gemma Scope made this universal for Gemma. Llama Scope (community-maintained) covers most open Llama models. Anthropic and OpenAI have internal equivalents. Training a one-off SAE for a paper is still possible and sometimes necessary, but it is no longer a contribution in itself. The contribution is what you do *with* the SAE.

This is healthy. It is the same maturation that happened to word embeddings in NLP a decade ago: the artifact stops being the research output and becomes the substrate. It also means the methodological decisions baked into the major SAE releases (the choice of expansion factor, the choice of sparsity penalty, the choice of layers to decompose) have become field-defining defaults that downstream work inherits without much scrutiny. I think we will eventually pay for some of these defaults. The defaults seem sensible right now but they are not optimized for any specific downstream task.

**Safety applications are real but narrow.** The most frequent question I get asked about my own work, from outside the field, is whether interpretability is producing operational safety value. The 2026 answer is "yes, in narrow regimes." Anthropic and OpenAI both run interpretability-derived monitors on subsets of their production traffic. The Arditi et al. refusal-direction work has produced robust monitors for refusal-related behavior across open-weight models. Several alignment-evaluation harnesses now incorporate feature-level probes as a complement to behavioral evals. None of this is comprehensive, and none of it provides anything resembling a guarantee, but it is more than I expected the field to deliver by mid-2026 when I read Sharkey et al. in early 2025.

The narrowness is the important caveat. Safety applications work for behaviors where the relevant features can be identified, validated, and continuously monitored. They do not work for behaviors that emerge from distributed circuits with no clean feature representation. They do not work for behaviors that the model generates only under distributional shift not present at SAE training time. The applications are real. The coverage is narrow. The gap between coverage and the surface area of model behavior we would want to monitor is, if anything, growing as models get more capable.

## What This Paper Got Right and Wrong as a Forecast

A survey is also implicitly a forecast: by listing problems as open, it predicts that the listed problems are the ones the field will work on next. Sharkey et al. is a useful object for evaluating that forecast.

**What it got right.** The emphasis on intervention as the primary methodology was correct. The flag on faithfulness as an open problem was correct, and remains the most important diagnosis the paper makes. The framing of interpretability as a precondition for safety rather than a nice-to-have was correct, and has held up under the increased capability scaling of 2025-2026. The distinction between methods, applications, and foundations was a useful taxonomy that has organized the way the field talks about its own structure.

**What it got partly wrong.** The paper underestimated the speed at which automated tooling would scale. The bottleneck in 2026 is verification, not labeling. The paper underestimated the difficulty of cross-architecture transfer. The paper overestimated the importance of probing as a research direction relative to intervention. The paper underestimated the dominance attribution graphs would assume as the standard artifact.

**What it missed.** The paper does not contain a serious treatment of the question that has, in retrospect, become the most important methodological question of 2026: how do you know that an attribution graph faithfully describes the model's behavior, as opposed to describing one of many possible decompositions that all account for the same input-output behavior? This is the *dual basis* problem in a generalized form. Two attribution graphs that produce the same output behavior on the training distribution can diverge on held-out inputs. Which one is "the circuit"? The paper gestures at this in the foundations section but does not give it the weight I think it deserves. By 2026 this has become the open problem that I would put at the top of any new survey.

## What I Would Add If I Wrote This Today

A different list. Three additions and one reframing.

**Addition: faithfulness as a numerical target.** The methodology I am building for my thesis is one instance of a more general need. The field needs a quantitative faithfulness measure that everyone agrees on, the way image classification has top-1 accuracy and language modeling has perplexity. We do not have one. A 2026 survey should put faithfulness scoring at the center of methods rather than the periphery, and it should specifically discuss what makes a faithfulness measure trustworthy as opposed to circular.

**Addition: the dual basis problem.** Several decompositions of the same model can produce the same behavioral predictions. Choosing among them is currently an aesthetic decision (does the decomposition look interpretable?) rather than a principled one. This is a live open problem and is going to stay open until the field develops a framework for ranking decompositions by something other than how readable their labels are.

**Addition: scaling to frontier models without ablating their training data.** Most published SAE and circuit work is on models in the 1B to 12B parameter range. Frontier models are an order of magnitude larger and have training data we do not have access to. The methodology that works on Gemma 2B does not straightforwardly scale to Gemini 2.5 Pro. The field needs research on what survives this scaling and what does not. Sharkey et al. mentions this in passing. It deserves a dedicated section.

**Reframing: the research-versus-deployment gap.** The paper treats interpretability as primarily a research field, with applications as a secondary section. By 2026 it is becoming clear that the deployment use cases (continuous monitoring, alignment auditing, model editing) impose constraints that pure research does not. A research-grade circuit analysis costs six figures. A deployment-grade interpretability monitor needs to run cheaply enough to be invoked on every inference. The methodologies that work for one do not necessarily work for the other. A 2026 survey should treat deployment as a first-class methodological constraint, not an afterthought.

## Why This Paper Is Worth Reading Anyway

Despite the 2026 corrections I have spent half this review writing, I think Sharkey et al. (2025) is the right entry point for anyone who wants to understand mechanistic interpretability as a field. Not because it is current, but because it is honest about being incomplete.

The pathology of survey papers is that they describe their field as if it were finished. Sharkey et al. does the opposite. The taxonomy is presented as provisional. The open problems are presented as open. The methodologies are presented as contested. Every section ends with a list of things the authors do not know, and those lists are the most useful part of the paper. If you read it as a research roadmap rather than a research summary, it is the right document to start from.

I also think it represents a kind of intellectual humility that the field needs to maintain. Mechanistic interpretability has made dramatic progress in 2025-2026. It would be easy to write a 2026 survey that overclaims, that frames every open problem as a tractable engineering question, and that downplays the foundational uncertainties that remain. Sharkey et al. did not do that in 2025, and I hope the next coalition document, whenever it lands, does not do that either.

What I take away from rereading this paper at June 2026 is that the field is in the right shape to do the next round of work. The tools are sufficient. The taxonomy is sufficient. The community is large and competent. What is needed now is rigor: faithfulness scores, dual-basis frameworks, deployment-grade methodologies, and a willingness to publish negative results when interventions do not survive distribution shift. Sharkey et al. does not solve these. It correctly identifies them as the next round.

When the next survey comes out, in 2027 or 2028, I want it to be able to cite this one as the moment when the field's open problems were first cleanly enumerated. I think it will.
