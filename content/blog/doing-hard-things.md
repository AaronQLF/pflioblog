# On Doing Hard Things

Let me be specific about what "hard" means here. I do not mean unpleasant. Lots of things are unpleasant without being hard. What I mean by hard is: requiring sustained effort in the presence of genuine uncertainty about whether you will succeed. The uncertainty is load-bearing in that definition. Without it, you are just doing something tedious.

Most people are not avoiding hard things because they are lazy. They are avoiding them because the cost-benefit calculation, run quickly and intuitively, comes out negative. The cost is visible and immediate. The benefit is abstract and deferred. This is a completely predictable outcome of how human cognition handles intertemporal tradeoffs, and it is also, I think, systematically wrong.

## What actually happens when you do something hard

The naive model is that you get better at the specific thing. You train for a marathon, you get better at running. You work through a difficult proof, you understand the material better. That is true, but it is not the interesting part.

The more important thing that happens is that your model of your own capability updates. Before you have done something genuinely hard, your estimate of what you can handle is extrapolated from your prior experience, which is necessarily bounded by the hard things you have actually attempted. This creates a feedback loop with an obvious bias: the less you attempt, the more conservative your self-model becomes, which makes you less likely to attempt, and so on.

Doing something hard interrupts that loop. Specifically, it provides an out-of-distribution data point. You discover that you can tolerate more uncertainty, more failure, more sustained discomfort than your prior model predicted. That information generalizes. The next hard thing feels categorically different, not because it is easier, but because your model of your own capacity to endure it is better calibrated.

## The thing about not feeling ready

There is a very consistent phenomenology to starting something hard. It feels like you do not yet have whatever you need to do it. The feeling is that you need more preparation, more confidence, more of something, before you can begin properly.

This feeling is not a reliable signal. Or rather, it signals something real, which is that the task is actually hard, but it is not informative about whether you can do it. The feeling is present at the beginning of almost every hard thing I have done, including ones I eventually finished without incident.

I do not have a clean explanation for why people treat this feeling as informative when it demonstrably is not. My best guess is that in easy tasks, unfamiliarity and inability are correlated, so the feeling of not being ready is actually predictive. Hard tasks break that correlation. But the heuristic persists.

## What comfort actually costs

The opportunity cost of staying comfortable is not usually framed precisely enough to be useful.

It is not that you fail to grow or fail to develop resilience or whatever the usual formulation is. The more specific claim is this: you lose access to information about yourself that is only available in conditions of genuine difficulty. What you do when things are going wrong, how you respond to extended uncertainty, how your thinking degrades or holds up under pressure, these are not things you can observe about yourself under ordinary circumstances. They require conditions that comfortable living systematically avoids.

That kind of self-knowledge turns out to be practically useful. It determines how much you can rely on yourself in situations that matter. If you have no data from hard situations, your self-model in those situations is running on extrapolation, which is often wrong in the direction of underestimation.

## Why I keep choosing harder options

I want to be honest that this is not some principled commitment I operate from. It is closer to a pattern I have noticed empirically.

When I look at the decisions I am most satisfied with, they disproportionately cluster around times I did the harder thing when the easier option was available. When I look at the decisions I regret, they disproportionately cluster around the opposite. This is a stable pattern over enough data points that I now treat it as a prior.

This won't apply to everyone. But I suspect it applies to more people than most would guess, and that the reason most people do not notice the same pattern in their own history is that they have not looked at it with enough precision.
